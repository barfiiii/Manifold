SECTION 10

From polymers to a latent map

No background needed. We start with “what is a polymer?”, build up to how a molecular-dynamics simulation works, turn it into a table of numbers, and end with a machine-learning map that spots a structural transition. Each part is short; try the little demos as you go.

1What is a polymer?

A monomer is a small molecule that can link to others. A polymer is many monomers joined into one long chain, like beads on a string or paper clips in a chain. “Poly” means many.

A chain with 25 units is a 25-mer; 100 units, a 100-mer. The number of units is the degree of polymerisation. Plastics, rubber, DNA, proteins, cotton and the shell of a crab are all polymers.

2Structure decides behaviour

Two plastics made of the same monomer can behave very differently, depending on how the chains are arranged.

Linear

One chain, no branches. Chains slide past each other.

Branched

Side chains stop chains packing tightly.

Cross-linked

Chains tied together: a network (rubber, gels).

  • Backbone: the main chain of atoms. Side groups: pieces hanging off it.
  • Amorphous chains are tangled like cooked spaghetti; crystalline regions are neatly packed.
  • Hydrogen bonds are weak sticky links between chains (often O–H and N–H groups). Many of them make a polymer stiff and hard to melt.

3Our example: chitosan

Chitosan comes from the shells of crabs and shrimp. It is a chain of glucosamine rings joined by oxygen bridges (glycosidic oxygens). It carries NH2 and OH groups that form many hydrogen bonds, and it is used in wound dressings, drug delivery and hydrogels.

Chitosan structure: Wikimedia Commons, public domain (CC0). If it does not load, a simplified drawing appears.

Why this matters here. The study follows the backbone glycosidic oxygens. They are the same atoms in every chain, so each one can be tracked as a measurement point along the chain.

The dataset uses chains of 25, 50 and 100 units (the 25-, 50- and 100-mers) so you can ask: does chain length change what we see?

4Temperature and the glass transition

At low temperature the chains are nearly frozen: the material is hard and glassy. Heat it and the chains gain energy to wiggle and slide: it becomes soft and rubbery. The temperature of that change is the glass transition temperature, Tg.

Free volume is the empty space between chains. Think of a crowded room versus a dance floor: once there is room, people (chains) can move.

The classic way to find Tg: plot volume against temperature. The slope changes at Tg.

The catch. This needs you to know that volume is the thing to watch. Machine learning will look at the whole structure and show where things change, with no such assumption.

5Molecular dynamics (MD): a movie of atoms

We cannot watch atoms in a lab, so we simulate them. MD works like a loop:

  • Force field: rules for how atoms push and pull (bonds act like springs, atoms repel when too close).
  • Newton’s laws: from the forces, compute each atom’s acceleration, then move it a tiny timestep (about a femtosecond, 10-15 s). Repeat millions of times.
  • Temperature is the average motion of the atoms. A thermostat keeps it at the value you choose.
  • Equilibration: run until the system forgets its starting structure; then save snapshots (the trajectory).

6From atoms to numbers

ML needs a table. So each snapshot must become numbers. We use the Voronoi cell volume: the region of space closer to an atom than to any other. A big cell means a loosely packed neighbourhood; a small one, tight packing. Move the slider (it plays the role of heat jiggling the atoms) and watch the cells spread out.

7From a table to a map

Why reduce dimensions?

625 columns means each temperature is a point in 625-dimensional space, which nobody can look at. Dimensionality reduction squeezes it to 2-D while keeping what matters: points that were similar stay close.

Scale to 0–1 first

We apply global MinMax: the smallest value in the whole matrix becomes 0, the largest 1, everything else in between. Relative sizes stay intact, and no single large-valued column can dominate.

PCA, the shadow idea

PCA finds the direction along which the data varies most (PC1), then the next one at right angles (PC2). Like choosing the best angle to photograph a 3-D object so its shadow shows the most detail. t-SNE and UMAP are non-linear alternatives that preserve neighbourhoods.

8Check yourself

Why is temperature not used as a feature?

It would hand the answer to the model. We only use it afterwards to colour the points.

In a 17 × 625 matrix, what is a sample and what is a feature?

A sample is one temperature (a row). A feature is one backbone atom’s Voronoi volume (a column).

PCA on 16 samples shows 100% variance in 2 components. Good news?

No. It means the data has only two independent directions, which usually means it was generated by a 2-factor model rather than measured. Real simulation data has noise in many directions.

A map shows a bend near 390 K. Can we call that Tg?

Only with a second check, such as the volume–temperature curve from the same runs, and ideally repeats with different random seeds. One run per temperature has no error bar.

What do t-SNE axes mean?

Nothing physical. Only which points sit near each other, and the overall shape of the path, can be read.
