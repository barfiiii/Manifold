SECTION 01

The curse of dimensionality

Adding more measurements to your data sounds like a free gift. It is not. Each new dimension makes space explode, empties out your data, and breaks the idea of “nearby”. Four short experiments, each one running live in your browser, show how.

1What is a dimension?

A dimension is one measurement taken on every item. A person described by height alone has 1 dimension. Add weight: 2. Add age, blood pressure, and so on.

In our polymer study, one temperature is described by the Voronoi volume of 625 atoms, so each sample is a point in a 625-dimensional space. You cannot picture it, but the maths works the same way as in 2-D and 3-D, and some surprising things happen as the number grows.

2Experiment 1: space becomes empty

Split every axis into 5 slices. In 1-D that makes 5 boxes, in 2-D 25, in 3-D 125. Now scatter 400 random points and count how many boxes contain at least one point. Drag the slider.

2
Why it hurts. A model learns by seeing examples near the case it must handle. In high dimensions almost every box is empty, so almost every new case has no neighbours at all. To keep the same coverage you need exponentially more data.

3Experiment 2: “near” and “far” stop meaning anything

Pick 150 random points and measure every pairwise distance. The histogram shows each distance divided by the average distance. In low dimensions distances vary a lot. Watch what happens as you add dimensions.

2
Why it hurts. Many methods (k-nearest neighbours, clustering, t-SNE, UMAP) rely on “this point is closer than that one”. When the farthest point is barely farther than the nearest, that signal fades. This is one reason we reduce dimensions before running t-SNE.

4Experiment 3: all the volume hides in the corners

Put a ball inside a cube that just fits it. In 2-D the circle fills 79% of the square. What fraction of the cube does the ball fill in higher dimensions?

2
Why it hurts. In high dimensions nearly all of the space is “corner”, far from the centre where typical data would sit. Our intuition about space, built in 2-D and 3-D, misleads us.

5What it means for your polymer data

With n samples in d dimensions, the number of points per axis that fill the space evenly is about n1/d. Enter your own numbers.

Less than 2 points per axis means the data covers the space very thinly. That is why, with 17 samples and 625 features, we do not train a model that needs coverage. We reduce dimensions instead: PCA, t-SNE and UMAP ask which few directions carry the structure.

  • Reduce features (PCA, selecting informative atoms).
  • Get more samples, for example finer temperature steps.
  • Use simple models and always check with a baseline.
Careful. Dimensionality reduction is not magic: with very few samples a 2-D map can look convincing and still be noisy. Check against the physics (Section 10) and repeat with different seeds.

6Check yourself

A 1-D line is cut into 10 pieces. How many boxes do you need for 10 dimensions?

1010, ten billion. Boxes multiply with every dimension.

1000 points feel dense in 2-D. How many keep the same density in 5-D?

About 31.6 million. That is 31.6 points per axis (the square root of 1000) raised to the 5th power.

What happens to distances in very high dimensions?

They bunch together: the nearest and farthest points become almost equally far, so “closest” carries little information.

A circle fills 79% of its square. What about a 20-D ball in its cube?

About 0.0000025%. Almost the entire volume sits in the corners.

Why is “salt in the ocean” a good picture?

The amount of salt (your data) stays the same, but the space it must fill grows so large that it is hopelessly diluted.

Why does dimensionality reduction exist?

To squeeze many measurements into a few that keep the structure, which fights emptiness, makes distances meaningful again, and lets us see the data.
